## 1. Data quality — what we found and how we handled it

See `reports/data_quality_report.md` (auto-generated by `src/pipeline.py`) for the full
line-by-line log. Summary:

- **387 duplicate sales records** were removed.
- **132 rows** had a negative units-sold value (a data-entry error) — corrected to 0 rather
  than dropped, so no sales history was lost.
- **1,312 missing unit prices** were filled using that product's own median price; **1,294
  missing revenue values** were recomputed from units sold × price, rather than guessed.
- **Category labels were inconsistent** ("Furniture" / "furniture" / "FURNITURE") and have
  been standardized to 5 clean categories.
- **199 sales rows (0.15%)** occurred before that product's first inventory snapshot existed —
  these have no stock position and are excluded from risk scoring rather than estimated.

None of these issues were large enough to distort the analysis, but all are logged so the
client can audit exactly what was changed and why.

## 2. Key business insights

**1. Furniture is the leading category, but not by a wide margin.**
Furniture drives 27.3% of total revenue, followed closely by Small Appliances (22.9%) and
Decor (21.8%). Revenue is fairly spread across categories — no single category dominates,
which is useful context for the risk-scoring stage: an overstock problem in one category is
unlikely to be a company-wide cash crunch.

**2. Demand is meaningfully seasonal.**
The peak month (June) sees roughly **2x** the average daily demand of the lowest month
(September). Any forecast that ignores month-level seasonality will systematically
under-order ahead of peak periods and over-order into the trough — this is precisely why the
brief requires beating a seasonal-naive baseline, not just any baseline.

**3. Promotions have a large, measurable effect.**
Average units sold jump by **~83%** on promotion days compared to non-promotion days. This
is too large to ignore — `promo_flag` must be included as a forecasting feature, and the
executive readout should note that a chunk of "demand" is promotion-driven, not organic.

**4. Holidays lift demand too, but less than promotions.**
Holidays show a **~37%** lift over regular days — smaller than the promo effect, but still
material enough to include as a calendar feature in the model.

**5. 20 SKUs (10%) are dead-stock candidates.**
These are the bottom decile by total units sold across the full history. They are strong
early candidates for the "Markdown / Clear" quadrant in Section 08's risk grid, worth
flagging to Merchandising even before the formal risk-scoring model is built.

## 3. Data-quality caveat for the forecasting stage

Because 199 rows have no matching inventory snapshot, and lead times were imputed for ~197
inventory rows where they were missing, the risk-scoring step (D4) should treat those
specific SKU-date combinations as lower-confidence and consider excluding them from
stockout/overstock alerts rather than acting on an imputed value.
